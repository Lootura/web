Republicans don’t have a clear boogeyman for this year’s midterms

Republicans have tried to find a new boogeyman this year as they rail against Democratic socialism and the “radical” left: Zohran Mamdani. Alexandria Ocasio-Cortez. Bernie Sanders. Still more mentioned than all of them, at least in campaign ads: former President Joe Biden. A POLITICO review of hundreds of ads running in battleground House and Senate races in recent months shows Republicans largely haven’t seized on progressive icons in paid media. Instead, Biden is the Democrat most likely to be mentioned in GOP ads this cycle, coming up across dozens of races. President Donald Trump has frequently sought to blame Biden for high prices and other challenges that his administration has faced. Even so, the former president has become a far less useful punching bag for Republicans since leaving office and remaining largely out of the spotlight: At this time in the 2024 cycle, Biden had been mentioned in more than 1,000 Republican House and Senate ads, according to AdImpact data. The data illustrates how Republicans have been unable to land on a clear villain as they seek to convince voters that Democratic control of Congress would be worse than keeping the GOP in charge. Their warnings about old establishment figures like Biden and former Speaker Nancy Pelosi don’t land like they once did. But they also haven’t been able to seize on emerging democratic socialists like they had hoped to. Democrats’ foil, meanwhile, has never been more obvious, with Trump’s approval rating in the mid-thirties amid continued high prices and the Iran war. “No one will match the level of Pelosi for Republicans, in part because the Obamacare vote was such a touchstone moment. And the ‘we have to pass it to find out what’s in it,’” said Doug Heye, a longtime Republican operative who noted he still has “Fire Pelosi” swag from the Republicans’ successful 2010 midterm rout. The then-speaker was central to the GOP’s campaign message that year. Part of the challenge for Republicans comes from Democrats’ lack of a clear leader . Pelosi stepped down from Democratic leadership at the start of 2023, and her successor, House Minority Leader Hakeem Jeffries (D-N.Y.) does not elicit the same animus from Republicans. In the 2022 cycle, her last term as Democratic leader, Pelosi was invoked in more than 80 GOP ads, POLITICO’s analysis found. Now, she’s mentioned just a handful of times, while Jeffries hasn’t been mentioned in any battleground TV ads this year, according to the POLITICO review of transcripts. Biden was an easy target for the GOP in 2022 and 2024, when Republicans blamed him for high inflation and the cost of living. Now, poll after poll shows that voters are more likely to hold Trump — not Biden — responsible for the current state of the U.S. economy, even as Trump tries to redirect the blame. Even Kamala Harris, the most recent Democratic standard-bearer as their 2024 nominee, is named less often in paid media this cycle than Biden. And among other potential future Democratic presidential candidates, only California Gov. Gavin Newsom and Ocasio-Cortez appear as Republican foils — and not as often as Harris or Biden. Ocasio-Cortez is often mentioned by Republicans in the same sentence as Sanders, especially when the GOP seeks to tie Democrats in battleground races to the party’s progressive wing. In Michigan’s hotly contested Senate race, former public health official Abdul El-Sayed was endorsed by both — something his opponent Mike Rogers and GOP outside groups have seized on. “El Sayed is endorsed by extreme liberals like AOC, Elizabeth Warren, and Bernie Sanders,” one recent ad from the conservative super PAC Sentinel Action Fund says. Some Republicans have sought to run against the progressives even when there are no such ties: One ad that ran earlier this year from Texas Gov. Greg Abbott and the state’s GOP comptroller promised to stand up to Ocasio-Cortez, Sanders and the “new radical left.” Neither Sanders nor Ocasio-Cortez have endorsed in either race. Most often, the GOP has run ads seeking to tie Democratic candidates to socialism or communism — the concepts themselves — in dozens of House battleground races and nearly every major Senate race. Just this week, the Trump administration launched a national $20 million DHS-funded campaign-style ad spree that features Trump lambasting “communism, socialism, and Marxism.” But when it comes to specific political figures — like Mamdani, New York City’s mayor — it gets complicated. Republicans once salivated at the prospect of Mamdani as a foil when he was first elected last year. But his first year in office has largely boosted his standing with voters, complicating efforts to turn his tenure into a negative for Democrats. Trump’s surprising continued embrace of the New York City mayor — just last week, the president stood by Mamdani and said he hoped he would “be a great mayor” — has further complicated GOP efforts to paint him as a villain. In a handful of swingy New York congressional districts, Republicans have still attempted to tie their opponents to Mamdani, while Democrats have pushed back on the connection. In New York’s 21st District, which Trump won by more than 20 points two years ago, Democratic dairy farmer Blake Gendebien ran an ad arguing that his Republican opponent wants voters to think he’s “running against Zohran Mamdani” but “that’s a load of bull.” The GOP has characterized prominent Democrats as socialist for decades. As progressives won a series of contested primaries this year, some Democrats worried the rise of figures with ties to the Democratic Socialists of America would give Republicans easy ammunition for the general election. But being called a “socialist” may not have the same shock value it once did. “I don’t think just calling a candidate ‘socialist’ works that well for Republicans, particularly this late in the game,” said Matt Towery, a longtime GOP pollster. “If a candidate focuses on parts of the DSA’s platform and can authentically pin it on their opponent, that might work. But Republicans just assume people have a bad reaction to the term socialist, and the term doesn’t elicit the same reaction it used to.”

Republicans know they have to spend big to save Ken Paxton. They’re increasingly worried MAGA Inc. won’t throw down in time. In private conversations over recent weeks, senior Hill Republicans and GOP officials have nudged President Donald Trump’s team to start spending more on the Texas Senate race before it’s too late, according to three people with direct knowledge of those conversations, who were granted anonymity to discuss them. Those fears escalated after audio leaked of Paxton privately trashing Trump’s midterm convention, with some worrying it could impact whether MAGA Inc. continues spending after a current wave of GOP ad bookings run out Oct. 12. A Trump operative told POLITICO that MAGA Inc. and its affiliates are expected to spend near $45 million on direct mail, TV, and digital advertising in Texas through Oct. 12, an increase from its previous $20 million to boost Paxton. But they said the team is still assessing how much more money to send in the final weeks, in consultation with Senate Republicans and their top super PAC, the Senate Leadership Fund. The Trump operative, who like others in this article was granted anonymity to speak candidly, would not commit to any plans when asked by POLITICO. With national Democrats starting to finally spend for their Senate nominee, James Talarico, after months of holding off, and as polling of the race tightens, Republicans expect the cost of salvaging Paxton’s campaign will only skyrocket. And, they’ve started to panic. “It’s a five-alarm fire, no doubt,” one GOP senator said. “Can we put it out? I hope so.” “This is a slow-moving car crash that we're witnessing right now,” a Republican strategist in the state said. “And there's a 75% chance that the ultimate result here will be catastrophic from a GOP Senate majority standpoint.” A fourth Republican, a national operative, spoke to the urgency factor: “While it’s money we shouldn’t have needed to spend, it needs to be spent now,” said a national GOP operative, describing the state as more winnable than North Carolina, Georgia and potentially Ohio. With the MAGA Inc. money and additional GOP ad reservations, the race is already on track to top $200 million from outside Republican groups hoping to save Paxton. Paxton’s own campaign recently announced a $20 million ad buy — one of the largest single media investments in a Texas Senate race. Some Republicans want MAGA Inc. to take over spending for Paxton in Texas in the final few weeks, so SLF can focus its resources elsewhere. MAGA Inc. could also funnel money into SLF’s recently established Texas PAC. But, according to two people with direct knowledge of that matter, that hasn’t happened. “At some point I think the people spending that money are going to have to make hard choices and figure out where the money can have the biggest impact, and certainly Texas is the most expensive,” Sen. John Cornyn, who lost to Trump-backed Paxton in the primary, recently told POLITICO . For example, he crossed one of the brightest red lines with Trump, telling a group of donors that “everybody’s numbers dropped” after the president’s GOP convention in Dallas last month, according to the audio leak reported by The New York Times. “There seems to be very little strategy there,” said another Republican senator, granted anonymity to candidly discuss the race. “Sometimes, I think [Paxton] wants to lose.” The White House did not respond to a request for comment. Trump, while on his way to Texas to tour a motor facility on Thursday, told reporters he hadn’t talked to Paxton about his convention comments. Later, he told a crowd in Texas that “Ken Paxton was at my side more than anybody, and I have to always remember that because he can be a pain in the ass sometimes. I’ll be honest.” Sen. Ted Cruz (R-Texas) told reporters this week that he hadn’t seen the audio of Paxton trashing Trump’s convention. We’ve never had a problem in Texas. Let’s focus on taking back a seat we haven’t had in years,” said an ally of former Sen. John E. Sununu, who’s in a tight race against Democratic Rep. Chris Pappas. “There are real chances in Michigan and New Hampshire,” said the person, who was granted anonymity to speak candidly about the dynamics. Sununu’s campaign did not respond to a request for comment. Latcham, who heads SLF, said that “Texas PAC is a dedicated effort that will not take resources from our other ongoing investments across the Senate map.” Recent polling still shows the Texas Senate race with a razor-thin margin. Most public polling over the past two months shows Talarico with an edge, though Paxton’s campaign released an internal poll Tuesday that shows Paxton leading by three points among likely voters, 48% to 45%. “I don't think the needle has moved in the last month,” said one Texas Republican elected official, granted anonymity to speak frankly. “Unless it's the worst polling that's ever been done in political history, all this spend hasn't changed anything, and I think people have made their mind up." Rebecca Schneid TIME 3 h ago fresh drop

The G7 Is Releasing Emergency Fuel Reserves. How Much Will It Help Americans?

A sign displays the prices of unleaded gasoline and diesel fuel as a driver refuels a vehicle with diesel fuel at a G&M gas station in Torrance, California, US, on Monday, Sept. 28, 2026. —Caroline Brehman—Bloomberg The Group of Seven (G7) countries said Friday that the nations would release 100 million barrels of oil and fuel products in the coming months, starting with “substantial” amounts of diesel . The move comes amid a global shortage largely attributed to slowdowns of the passage of oil through the Strait of Hormuz. A joint statement from the G7 nations—Canada, France, Germany, Italy, Japan, the United Kingdom, and the U.S.—confirmed that the International Energy Agency (IEA) will coordinate the release over the next four months. It calls the move a part of “decisive, coordinated measures to stabilize immediate energy supplies” amid record-high fuel prices driven by the war in Iran and the recent escalation of fighting between Russia and Ukraine . Read More: Once Again, Energy Is Power “Europe has just agreed to release a massive amount of their heavily stocked Diesel Oil,” President Donald Trump wrote on Truth Social on Friday. “The process will begin immediately.” In talks leading up to the announcement on Friday, Trump had been pushing European partners for the release, citing high fuel prices for Americans. Last month, the Trump Administration threatened to temporarily suspend its exports of diesel—earning immediate backlash from countries that rely on it, including Canada, Mexico, and much of Europe. European leaders and oil executives at the time said that such a ban would be disastrous for long-term oil flow. For that reason, experts believe the threat helped pressure those nations to release their fuel reserves. The agreement could offer some relief for Americans even if the released diesel stays in Europe, because Europe buys some of its diesel from the U.S. Releasing European reserves could ease competition for U.S. diesel and potentially reduce prices for American buyers. This is especially critical as the cost of Brent crude oil sits at $102 per barrel as of Oct. 3 and on-highway diesel hit a high of $6.529 per gallon as of last month, according to the U.S. Energy Information Administration . But how much relief consumers will actually see—and how quickly—will depend on what type of fuel is released, how much reaches the market and when, and whether supply disruptions persist. What is actually being added to the market? Although the G7 shared how much fuel it would release, it did not specify the full breakdown of the release by fuel type. Its statement did say, however, that there will be a “frontloaded” and substantial release of diesel fuel within the first 20 days. While crude oil must be refined into products like gasoline before consumers can use it, diesel is already a finished fuel. That means a diesel release could address the immediate shortage more directly. The planned release could help ease shortages of both crude oil and refined fuels, with supplies disrupted by the wars in Iran and Ukraine. The supply of crude oil has been heavily disrupted this year after the U.S. and Israel launched joint strikes that ignited a war with Iran in February. In response, Iran effectively closed the Strait of Hormuz in March. More recent negotiations to end the war have largely failed to address differing opinions on ownership and control over the Strait. This has critically restricted the passage of oil-carrying vessels through the waterway, with daily shipping traffic in the single digits last month, down from pre-war averages of roughly 138 to 140 daily transits. Diesel, meanwhile, is essential to U.S. transportation, agriculture, and manufacturing: Most goods, food, and manufacturing supplies across the country are transported by heavy-duty trucks and trains running on diesel engines. The war in Iran has driven diesel prices up and lowered supply, but Russia and Ukraine’s targeting of one another’s energy infrastructure has further exacerbated the issue. While the U.S. in particular does not heavily rely on Russian diesel, America is vulnerable to the volatility caused by Russia removing it from the global market. Trump has claimed outright that Ukraine’s targeting of Russian oil infrastructure is to blame for diesel shortages. What could Americans feel, and when? Jason Bordoff, founding director of the Center on Global Energy Policy at Columbia University, says it’s difficult to predict the consumer impact given the scarce details shared so far. “It makes a big difference if the release is in the next two weeks or if it's four months from now,” Bordoff says. “I think if we saw some announcement tomorrow from Germany or France that gave concrete, specific numbers for a substantial amount—50 million barrels or so—of diesel, then it could have a real impact if this release actually happens in a timely fashion,” Bordoff says. At the same time, Jeff Colgan, a professor of political science at Brown University, cautions that American consumers may see limited benefits. “Releasing diesel could make a difference on price, especially in tight markets in Europe,” Colgan says. “But American consumers are unlikely to see much of a price drop from this policy move unless the diesel share of that 100 million barrels is very large.” With a timely release of diesel, Bordoff predicts that Americans could see a drop by as much as 25 cents per gallon after a few weeks—but, he adds, the markets seem pretty unsure that that will actually happen. And market perception is central to whether or not prices drop in the short term. “I think they’re saying: Show me the barrels, so I have confidence this is real, and it's going to happen quickly,” he says. David Bieri, an economist and associate professor of urban affairs and planning at Virginia Tech, tells TIME that international interventions like this help “signal” to markets that governments are a credible safety net in prolonged periods of uncertainty. “That's the main mechanism, because they want to influence market sentiment,” Bieri says. “Then, futures markets begin to price more optimistically, and then eventually the hope is that prices will come down.” The effect Bieri is describing has already begun: The price of diesel futures in the United States fell 8% on Friday morning, a sign of more optimism in the market. Bordoff adds that they are also, in part, a response to Trump dropping his threat of a diesel export ban —a threat that was lifted as a condition of the G7 agreement. The release itself may not impact the markets “an awful lot,” Bieri says, given the broader context of the war with Iran. “In fact, consumers might not necessarily notice it, ever. That's how insignificant the quantity itself is,” he says of the 100 million barrels. “But it's the importance of the cooperative signal that hopefully will make things better this fall.” Bieri also cautions that any savings could take time to reach consumers, as carriers and other distributors may use lower fuel costs to rebuild their margins. “[They] have been bleeding on increased fuel costs,” he says. “This is going to be, if anything, a welcome reprieve for their profit margins, and they're not going to pass that on immediately to consumers.” What happens if the supply gap is still there when releases taper off? This October announcement is not the first of its kind: Following the initial market shock of the war in Iran, IEA member countries announced a 400-million-barrel release in March—80% of which has already been released. Notably, the latest announcement did not clearly establish how much of the forthcoming release would be additional to earlier commitments. In March , the IEA said that the majority of the barrel release would be crude oil; however, reports show that Europe has primarily contributed refined oil, like diesel—which could suggest more of the same in this latest round. In either event, such fuel releases are just a matter of sticking a bandage over the larger issue of two unresolved wars and continued global energy disruption, says Bordoff. “We're going to continue to have oil, gasoline, and diesel price shocks until that conflict comes to some sort of resolution, and energy flows can get going again,” he says. “Inventories, and stocks and other schemes like waiving state fuel taxes or something can help on the margin for temporary disruptions, but at some point this isn't temporary anymore.” Michael Noel, economist at Texas Tech University, agrees with Bordoff, telling TIME that “unless something dramatic happens to end the Ukraine and Iran conflicts, the supply shortage will persist.” Negotiations between Iranian leadership and the Trump Administration have stalled multiple times in the last six months. Trump said on Sept. 9 that he expected a resolution to come after the midterm elections, which take place in the U.S. on Nov. 3. “I think the war's going to end immediately after the election because they can't hold out any longer,” he said last month. “Right after the election, oil prices are going to be tumbling downward.” Axios reported , however, that senior Trump Administration officials met at Camp David to discuss the war on Friday. The talks come as the U.S. last week added economic pressure in the region with a series of sanctions aiming to curtail funding streams and stymy industrial lifelines for Iran. Experts argue that the fuel crisis and volatility exposes the need for the U.S. to adapt to a changing energy ecosystem. Notably, Colgan says that would include “the national security advantages of clean technologies like EVs , batteries, and renewable energy.”
