    Democrats Offer a Road Map to Their Investigations of Trump The Atlantic · 15.9 LOOTS contributed
    Representative Robert Garcia , the likely incoming chair of the House Committee on Oversight and Government Reform, has spent months writing names on a whiteboard in his California home, tallying the figures in President Trump’s orbit he wants to investigate. Today, he reveals who tops the list. Garcia is sending a dozen letters to high-ranking Trump-administration officials and members of the president’s family, providing the clearest signal yet of which topics—and which people—will likely face the greatest scrutiny once Democrats take control of the House. His targets include two of the president’s sons (Donald Trump Jr. and Eric Trump) and his son-in-law Jared Kushner; his former White House chief of staff Susie Wiles and current one, Stephen Miller; Vice President Vance and Attorney General Todd Blanche, as well as the former DHS officials Kristi Noem and Corey Lewandowski. The letters inform their recipients of the need to preserve records, a common legal maneuver that usually precedes more aggressive action. The letters mention the “sweeping mandate” from voters in the midterms and are largely focused on three areas: allegations of corruption against the Trump family, alleged misconduct at the Department of Homeland Security, and the White House’s decisions regarding the release of investigative files related to Jeffrey Epstein. Garcia is also asking the president of the nonprofit Trust for the National Mall to preserve records related to the funding and construction of the White House ballroom, suggesting that the committee might investigate whether special interests got anything in return for their hefty donations. Among those Garcia mentions specifically is Nvidia Corporation, which made its donation around the time that it received permission from the Trump administration to sell advanced microchips to China. The letters underscore just how contentious relations between the new Congress and the White House are likely to grow following two years in which Republican leaders on Capitol Hill studiously avoided any oversight of Trump-administration activities. White House Counsel Will Scharf has already accused Garcia of “abusive and capricious threats” and warned that some of his investigations “may cross the line into blatant criminality.” News of the targets will likely also prompt renewed conversations within the Democratic Party over how to use their large majority. Democrats are weighing how to strike a balance between the affordability issues that motivated many voters across the political spectrum and the desire for accountability that animated much of the party’s base. “This corruption is totally out of control,” Hakeem Jeffries, who is likely to become House speaker, said yesterday afternoon during The Atlantic Summit. “And we’ve said we’re going to hold the crooks accountable.” [ Read: Can Democrats hold Trump accountable? ] The letters being sent today to the president’s sons cite their business pursuits in areas that overlap with entities that may be seeking preferential treatment from the White House. A letter being sent to Kushner refers to his serving as an envoy in the Middle East, Russia, and Ukraine while also receiving “billions of dollars in fees and investments with particularly troubling ties to Middle East states where you also have a government role.” “Americans should be secure in the knowledge that their government is focused on their best interests—not on making the President’s family richer,” Garcia writes in each of the letters. “The American people have spoken, and they demand accountability.” Another letter is going to Kimberly Guilfoyle, the former U.S. ambassador to Greece—and former fiancée to Don Jr. It accuses her of engaging in pay-to-play schemes and using her position to advance potential business ties, allegations that ultimately led to her firing. (As with the other letter recipients, Guilfoyle has denied wrongdoing.) The letters ask for information and a response. Ultimately, they can lay the groundwork for a subpoena—though whether the administration will be willing to cooperate is unclear. A number of the letters that Garcia is sending relate to immigration enforcement, especially controversies within the Department of Homeland Security. The letter to Miller, who is described as the “lynchpin” and “architect” of immigration policies, is the most direct. The letter accuses Miller, who was promoted to chief of staff just after the midterm election, of aggressively pursuing quotas, overseeing large sweeps of immigrants, and encouraging heavy-handed enforcement from ICE officers. “Oversight Democrats have documented 1,165 instances of concerning uses of force, arrests or detentions, deportations, enforcement actions at sensitive locations, and acts of intimidation or retaliation by DHS personnel, and we want answers,” Garcia writes in the letter to Miller. Miller has vigorously defended the administration’s immigration policies, urging high-volume arrests and describing them as necessary to enforce the law. Garcia is also sending letters to Noem, the former secretary of homeland security, and to Lewandowski, one of her top aides. Those letters accuse the two of steering lucrative government contracts to political allies and donors. They were both ousted from their roles amid scandals that the committee wants to explore. One that is mentioned in the letter is Noem’s decision on her last day as secretary to approve the multimillion-dollar purchase of five airplanes from a company chaired by a political donor. Noem is no longer in the Cabinet, but the letter declares that her activities remain fair game. “Your employment status, whether with the federal government or not, does not affect the Oversight Committee’s ability to pursue investigation,” the letter states. Noem has denied reporting by The New York Times about the plane purchases. [ Read: Here’s who Democrats want to impeach next ] Garcia also centers the lingering controversy and intrigue over Epstein. In letters sent to Vance and Wiles, he requests information about meetings held in the White House Situation Room concerning the release of Epstein-related investigative files. Vance and Wiles are asked to identify every meeting that administration officials held concerning the disgraced financier. In a letter sent to Blanche, Garcia asks him to preserve records concerning the decision to interview Ghislaine Maxwell while she was serving a prison sentence, and then to transfer her . “This interview has raised questions about a potential quid pro quo, given that a few days after Maxwell praised the President, his Administration transferred her to a much more comfortable minimum-security prison camp,” he writes. The letters make clear that Garcia is attempting to force top Trump-administration officials to answer potentially uncomfortable questions. Trump and his aides are unlikely to be cooperative witnesses, however, which could raise the prospect of using subpoena power to compel their testimony. Those who don’t comply with a subpoena could be referred to the Department of Justice—but a Blanche-led department is unlikely to take the matter any further, much less prosecute, for example, Blanche himself. “This is just the beginning,” Garcia said in a statement accompanying the letters. With Democrats eagerly eyeing their expanded powers and Trump offering his political opponents no shortage of material to investigate, that much is surely true. *Illustration sources: Mary Evans / Bridgeman Images; Marwan Naamani / AFP / Getty; Hulton Fine Art Collection / Getty; PHAS / Universal Images / Getty.

    Scoop: White House drops Heidi Overton's FDA nomination Axios · 7.9 LOOTS contributed
    The White House is dropping Heidi Overton 's nomination to lead the Food and Drug Administration because of a lack of congressional support, Axios has learned. Why it matters: The decision means the Trump administration will have to find a new candidate to lead the FDA as HHS Secretary Robert F. Kennedy Jr. looks to advance his Make America Healthy Again agenda. "FDA nominee Heidi Overton lacks the support on the Hill to move forward with her confirmation," a senior administration official told Axios late Tuesday. Zoom in: Overton, a physician and deputy director of the White House Domestic Policy Council, was nominated by President Trump in August. She had drawn criticism from segments of the MAHA movement that saw her siding with health and agriculture interests and helping undermine Kennedy agenda. Antiabortion forces also questioned her commitment to tightening rules for prescribing the abortion pill mifepristone. The backstory: Her nomination faced opposition from Democrats and skepticism from some Republican senators, including Sen. Bill Cassidy (R-La.), chair of the Senate's Health, Education, Labor and Pensions (HELP) Committee. Cassidy said he had "strong concerns" about Overton's role in the administration's efforts to change childhood vaccine recommendations, which he called "almost disqualifying." What they're saying: "We need to go in a new direction at FDA, and everybody fully supports Secretary Kennedy's vision to Make America Healthy Again and get back to a more populist and common-sense approach to health in America," the senior administration official said. The White House had tried to shore up her nomination Monday, posting on social media an account from the former dean of the University of New Mexico School of Medicine supporting Overton's confirmation. What's next: The HELP committee had been scheduled to vote on Overton's nomination on Wednesday. The FDA has been led by acting Commissioner Kyle Diamantas since Marty Makary resigned in May.
