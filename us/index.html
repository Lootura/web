    Nvidia

    In the news for

    Chris Christie says Jensen Huang shouldn’t come back to Nvidia. Hours later, the board agreed Fortune · 5.4 LOOTS contributed
    On Tuesday morning, Jensen Huang said he’d return as Nvidia’s CEO if the board asked. By that afternoon, a possible 2028 Republican presidential candidate said he shouldn’t. A few hours later, Nvidia’s board said the same. “No,” former New Jersey Gov. Chris Christie said when Fortune’s Diane Brady asked at the Fortune 500 Innovation Forum whether Huang should return. Christie lumped the former Nvidia CEO in with President Trump as an example of “people in positions of power without any character.” Speaking onstage with former Democratic Chicago Mayor Rahm Emanuel at the Fortune 500 Innovation Forum Tuesday, Christie, who is a Republican, railed against Huang’s behavior. “We have gone on a character holiday for 10 years,” he said. “We have said—because we have a leader in the White House and leaders throughout business like Jensen Huang who have said all that matters is the bottom line, and character doesn’t matter anymore.” The White House did not immediately respond to Fortune ’s request for comment. Huang steps down Huang stepped down as CEO of Nvidia last week after admitting he was unfaithful to his wife, amid reports of more than one extramarital relationship. The New York Times previously reported on his close relationship with a woman U.S. intelligence services suspected was a Chinese spy. China has denied the allegation. Huang’s behavior, Christie implied Tuesday, was an example of the degradation of ethics that has taken over America during the past decade. President Trump was first elected president in 2016. Trump, who has forged a close relationship with Huang over the past year, defended the former tech executive, saying in response to a question from ABC Monday that he “would not mind” if Huang returned as CEO. The president has repeatedly expressed sympathy for the former Nvidia CEO, calling him a “tremendous talent” following his resignation. Notable business figures like Elon Musk have backed Huang’s return to the CEO position. Huang in his first public statement since he stepped down said he had received an “outpouring of calls” to return as CEO after he resigned, including from Nvidia’s employees and customers. “If the Board of Directors decides that my return is in the best interest of our shareholders, our employees, the company, and America, I would be honored to answer these calls and return as CEO,” he wrote in a post on X. Nvidia’s board, however, has remained steadfast. On Tuesday afternoon, interim CEO Colette Kress and Nvidia’s new chairman of the board Harvey Jones oversaw the company’s first earnings call without Huang, and made it clear he would not return. “The board does not intend to reinstate Jensen as CEO,” Jones said on the call. The company on Tuesday reported revenue of $115.8 billion for the three months ended Oct. 25, which was more than double what it reported during the same period last year and was above the $109 billion analysts expected . “We’ve got this,” Kress said during the call. Falling public trust Both Christie and Emanuel are weighing runs for the White House. In an interview with Politico earlier this month, Christie said he is still considering whether to run for President in 2028. He previously sought the Republican nomination for President in 2016 and 2024. Emanuel said during the WBUR festival in May that he was contemplating a presidential run in 2028. Political parties aside, the two split on Huang. Christie said Huang’s recent behavior fits in with the decline in ethics that, in his opinion, has taken hold in America over the past decade. “America is no longer being seen around the world as a leader you want to emulate, and I think that is tragic, but it can be fixed with new leadership in Washington at the White House that can do that,” Christie added. But Emanuel, who shared the stage with Christie Tuesday, took a more pragmatic view of Nvidia’s recent leadership shakeup. “Look, Nvidia is a separate issue, and I think the technology is what’s valuable,” Emanuel said. “There are other CEOs. The technology is what’s valuable,” he said. Still, Emanuel agreed with Christie that the loss of public trust goes beyond Washington to the private sector, and especially tech. “Don’t underestimate the lack of trust that CEOs in America have, the tech bros have,” he said. “This is not restricted just to the public sector.” This story was originally featured on Fortune.com
