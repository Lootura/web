    Donald Trump

    In the news for

    Why Trump Rescued Jensen Huang The Atlantic · 8.5 LOOTS contributed
    A presidency that operated by normal rules of ethics or even self-preservation would be scrambling to distance itself from the repercussions of the Nvidia scandal. Last night, The New York Times reported that a Chinese woman who had an affair with Jensen Huang, Nvidia’s once and future chief executive, was in regular contact with China’s spy agency . The woman, Alice Huang, may have influenced the Nvidia CEO’s decision to sell advanced chips to companies that transferred them to China. Donald Trump instead pushed to reinstate Jensen Huang as the company’s CEO, even going so far as to dial in to the company’s board meeting to push for Huang’s return, a demand the company was in no position to turn down. Trump’s decision is overdetermined, as an academic might put it, by several Trumpian precepts. The president believes as a matter of principle that powerful men should not face repercussions for sexual misconduct. He also takes a relativistic view of corruption, seeing it as simply a normal way to do business. This mindset allows him to routinely smear any opponent as corrupt (everybody does it, right?) while simultaneously engaging in flagrant corruption himself (after all, everybody does it). But perhaps the most significant Trumpian attribute at play is his disregard for the national interest . Nvidia was permitting its advanced chips to fall into Chinese hands. This may have violated American law, and the law serves a critical purpose: to prevent America’s most powerful global rival from gaining access to cutting-edge technology that might enable it to obtain economic and military dominance. [ Read: A $5.7 trillion face-plant ] Conservative Republicans, who tend to be the most hawkish politicians on China, are outraged by this, which may well turn out to have a decisive effect on the course of global leadership. The prospect of the U.S. losing the artificial-intelligence race to China out of an American businessman’s greed or libidinousness should leave them predictably furious. Trump, however, does not seem to care whatsoever. Despite his occasional demagoguery against China, he seems to have little interest in competing against it. He proclaims his admiration of its dictator, Xi Jinping, more than any other world leader save Vladimir Putin, the Russian dictator. After a symbolic but fruitless meeting with the two partners last week, Trump giddily posted memes depicting the troika as global leaders akin to Roosevelt, Stalin, and Churchill. Trump does not want China to exert global dominance, but he does not seem to view its ambitions with any concern. He told reporters last week that he talked about Huang with Xi: “I would love to see Jensen be given a second chance. You know, we’re a country of second chances and I’d love to be able to see him have—that’s a very important company to us. And he put us in a position where we are literally 10 years advanced over other companies, and I did discuss that with President Xi, and he said, Oh, brilliant, brilliant .” In other words, Trump seems to be saying that he asked the president of China, the country that is suspected of running the honey-trap operation on Huang, whether Trump should bring back the CEO of Nvidia. Then, Trump would have us believe, the Chinese president agreed that reinstating the executive over which China is feared to have leverage would be a good idea. And Trump presented this agreement as an encouraging sign. [ Read: China’s intelligence coup in the AI war ] In a normal administration, the mere fact that the president is telling a private company whom to install as CEO would itself constitute an enormous scandal. In the current atmosphere, this is simply a background detail. The most benign explanation for Huang’s restoration is that Trump cares more about keeping the stock market soaring than any American national-security interest. Less benign interpretations include the possibility that Trump’s inner circle has profited from Nvidia circumventing export controls. Determining what in fact took place will likely consume months of reporting and Watergate-style oversight hearings from a Congress that, as of January, will no longer be controlled by the president’s lackeys. Going into damage-control mode would be rational. Instead Trump apparently believes that he can train his supporters to align their instincts with his and define his behavior as good and correct, as he did with the Russia hoax, or the perfect phone call with Ukrainian President Volodymyr Zelensky, or the stolen 2020 election. “I have gotten to know Jensen, and he is a Great Patriot and National Treasure,” Trump wrote on Truth Social this morning. He followed this up by sharing a depiction of the 2024 attempted assassination of himself in Butler, Pennsylvania, but with Huang in his place, bloodied and holding aloft a red bra. As long as the stock market goes up, Jensen Huang can sleep with all the Chinese spies he wants.
