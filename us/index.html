    Anthropic

    In the news for

    Anthropic just set an IPO record nobody’s bragging about: $20 billion left on the table Fortune · 10.4 LOOTS contributed
    The Anthropic IPO just delivered Wall Street’s AI bulls a strong vote of confidence. The Claude-purveyor’s big jump on its opening day of trading for now dispelled fears that investors’ faith in the profitability of this era’s signature technology was fading. Here’s an issue that the bulls are ignoring, and that’s getting virtually no coverage in the media: Anthropic left an enormous share of the IPO’s proceeds on the table. We’re talking about roughly $20 billion that could have flowed into spending on AI infrastructure that instead went to big institutions, and privileged retail investors, that reaped a gigantic, one-day windfall, as well as the underwriters that will feast on big commissions they’ll receive from the clients they awarded shares at what looks like bargain prices. The “left on the table” phenomenon looks amazingly like the scenario surrounding the SpaceX debut in June, though all told, the Anthropic forgone funding number’s even bigger. So let’s start by comparing the two offerings. In total amount raised, Anthropic edged SpaceX as the biggest IPO of all time, collecting a headline, gross amount from the shares sold to the public of $81.34 billion versus $75 billion for SpaceX. For additional insights into the similarities and contrasts between the two IPOs, I spoke to Jay Ritter, professor at the University of Florida and the world’s leading expert in the field. First, Ritter noted that the percentage fees paid the underwriters are the two lowest numbers he’s ever witnessed, chiefly because the deals are so immense that even at what sounds like a tiny number, the dollars going to the underwriters are the biggest ever collected. The percentages are just a hair apart, 0.666% on SpaceX and 0.676% for Anthropic, a difference of just one-hundredth of 1%. All told, Anthropic is paying the syndicate led by Goldman Sachs $550 million versus $500 million for the SpaceX IPO, where Goldman also served as chief or “lead left” underwriter. Second, Ritter notes that Anthropic is following SpaceX in departing from the usual practice by paying no fees on the 15% “over allotment” shares reserved for the underwriters to sell at the offering price in case the issue is over-subscribed, as in both of these super-coveted IPOs. On a third issue, the two offerings diverge substantially. “Anthropic is unique having a large amount of shares held by employees and investors that have no lockup period at all so they can be sold immediately at the market price,” says Ritter. He notes that the ability of these investors to place shares on the market on day one creates extra liquidity that may smooth potential price volatility in early trading. On opening day, SpaceX shares jumped 19.2%. Hence, it collected $14.417 billion less than if it had sold at the price folks and funds were willing to pay—for example, had it deployed a “Dutch auction” instead of a traditional underwriting. That difference, Ritter notes, is known as the “amount left on the table,” or the forgone proceeds. By contrast, the Anthropic pop was slightly lower; shares rose from the offering price of $83 to $97.17 at the close, for a jump of 17.1%. But since Anthropic raised several billions more than SpaceX, the dollars left on the table figure was practically identical at $14.337 billion, or a mere $80 million less. That near tie doesn’t tell the whole story. In addition to the $81.34 billion offering to the public, Anthropic also conducted a private placement that sold $26 billion in its non-voting N shares to a group including Alphabet , Amazon and Nvidia . When sold, the N shares convert to the regular A voting category. Those legends of tech and other investors also paid 17.1% less than the market judged the shares were worth at day end. That’s an extra $4.4 billion left on the table. Next comes the over-allotment, also known as the “greenshoe.” Selling another 15% of the shares at 17.1% less than the price at the market close amounts to an additional $2 billion “discount” from the price at 4 PM on November 19. All told, the total comes to just over $20 billion. “Then you include everything, it’s the largest number left on the table in the history of IPOs,” says Ritter. It’s a superlative few on Wall Street are mentioning. This story was originally featured on Fortune.com
